Google Site Reputation Policy Changes in EEA: What SEOs Need to Know

Google will change how site reputation policy manual actions affect searchers in the European Economic Area from August 30, 2026. Here is what is changing and what publishers should watch.

By Deepak ParmarPublished August 28, 2026Updated August 29, 2026SEO update
Quick answer: From August 30, 2026, Google will change how site reputation policy manual actions work for searches in the EEA. Outside the EEA, affected sections can still receive the direct manual-action impact; inside the EEA, that direct impact will not apply, and Google may separate the affected section so it can rank independently over time.

Google has announced a major change to its site reputation policy. Starting August 30, 2026, a manual action under the policy will work differently for people searching from inside the European Economic Area (EEA) than it does for people searching from outside the EEA.

The announcement came from Google Search Central on August 28, 2026, following discussions with the European Commission. Google says it is adjusting its enforcement approach in the EEA while keeping the site reputation policy in place.

Key takeaway: This is mainly a change in enforcement, not a removal of the site reputation policy. Outside the EEA, the affected part of a site can still be directly impacted by a manual action. Inside the EEA, that direct impact will not apply, and Google may separate the affected section so it can rank independently over time.

What is Google's site reputation policy?

Google introduced the site reputation policy to address a specific form of search manipulation: publishing third-party content on a trusted website mainly because that website has strong ranking signals from its own content.

The basic concern is simple. A publisher can have a strong site in one topic, then allow unrelated or third-party content onto the same domain because the host site's reputation may help those pages rank better than they could on their own.

This is often discussed in SEO circles as “parasite SEO,” but Google's policy is the more useful reference because it defines the search policy rather than the industry nickname.

Google's current documentation should be the source of truth when evaluating a site. See the Google Search spam policies for the full rules. For the wider August rollout, see ToolboxKart's Google August 2026 spam update guide.

What changes on August 30?

Searcher locationEffect of a site reputation manual action
Outside the EEAThe manual action directly affects search results for the affected portion of the site. The rest of the site is not affected by that manual action.
Inside the EEAThe manual action's direct impact will not apply. Google says the affected section may be separated in its systems so it can rank independently from the rest of the site over time.

Google says this different treatment begins August 30, 2026. The EEA includes the European Union plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway.

Does this mean Google removed the policy in Europe?

No. That would be too broad a conclusion.

Google has not said that the practice is now acceptable in the EEA. It says the enforcement effect of a manual action is changing for people searching from the EEA. Google also says it remains committed to the policy because it believes the policy supports a more reliable search experience.

The distinction matters for publishers. The policy still describes a search quality problem. What changes is how a manual action affects search results based on the searcher's location.

What does “separated” mean for affected sections?

Google says that for users in the EEA, an affected section of a site may be separated in its systems so that, over time, it ranks independently from the rest of the domain.

That does not mean the section will automatically rank well. It means the section may no longer receive the same ranking benefit from the main site's established signals.

For example, imagine a strong publisher has an unrelated third-party content section. If that section falls under the site reputation policy, an EEA searcher may not see a direct manual-action demotion. But Google can still separate the section so its content has to earn visibility more independently.

Will the rest of the website be affected?

Google's explanation keeps the manual action focused on the affected portion of a site. Outside the EEA, the rest of the site will not be affected by that particular site reputation manual action.

Inside the EEA, the direct manual-action effect does not apply. However, publishers should not read this as a reason to ignore a problem section. A section that depends heavily on the main domain's authority may lose that advantage if Google separates it.

What happens to existing manual actions?

Google says site owners will continue to receive notifications in Search Console when a manual action is applied. If a site owner believes a manual action was made in error, Google says they can submit a reconsideration request.

Google also says eligible sites will, after the reconsideration request, have an opportunity to bring disputes to mediation.

This means Search Console remains important even with the regional change. If your site has a manual action, do not rely on a ranking check from one country to decide whether the problem exists.

What should publishers and SEOs do now?

1. Audit third-party content by purpose

Make a list of content published by outside contributors, commercial partners, affiliates, or other third parties. Ask why each section exists. If the main reason is to use the host domain's ranking strength, it deserves a closer review.

2. Check topical fit

Look for sections that are far away from the site's normal subject. A website can cover several topics and still be useful, so topic difference alone is not proof of a violation. The stronger signal is whether the section was placed on the site mainly to take advantage of the host site's ranking signals.

3. Review sponsored and partner content carefully

Commercial content needs a clear purpose and should not be treated as a shortcut to rankings. Review who creates the content, who controls it, why it is hosted on the domain, and whether the pages would make sense for users without the site's existing authority.

4. Check Search Console for manual actions

Open the Manual Actions report and check for current notices. If there is an action, read Google's stated reason instead of trying to infer it from ranking tools alone.

5. Compare EEA and non-EEA visibility

After August 30, regional comparison becomes more important. If you have a large European audience, track performance from EEA countries separately from markets outside the EEA.

Look at clicks, impressions, queries, pages, and search appearance. Do not treat one day's movement as proof of a policy effect. Build a trend across several days and weeks.

6. Keep your main site and partner sections clear

If your site hosts outside content, make the relationship easy to understand. Clear ownership, editorial responsibility, topic fit, and useful content are better signals than trying to hide how a section was created.

What this means for “parasite SEO”

The update is especially important for the SEO practice often called parasite SEO because it creates a regional difference in the enforcement outcome.

But the change should not be treated as a new strategy for ranking third-party content in Europe. Google explicitly says the site reputation policy remains important. The affected section may also be separated from the rest of the site's ranking signals.

For agencies and publishers, the safer approach is to build content that can earn visibility because it is useful and relevant, rather than because it sits on a domain with a strong history.

Why this update matters beyond Europe

This is an important SEO development because it shows that search policy enforcement can have different effects based on where a person searches from.

For international SEO teams, that makes regional reporting more valuable. A single global visibility number can hide changes that happen in one market.

It also reinforces a wider lesson: Google policies, enforcement systems, and search results are not always the same thing. SEOs need to separate the written policy, the enforcement mechanism, and the actual ranking data when investigating a change.

What should you monitor after August 30?

  • Manual action messages in Google Search Console.
  • Organic clicks and impressions from EEA countries.
  • Organic visibility outside the EEA for the same URLs.
  • Performance of third-party, sponsored, affiliate, or partner sections.
  • Changes to pages that depend strongly on the main domain's authority.
  • Any new Google guidance about site reputation enforcement.

Final take

Google's August 28, 2026 announcement changes how site reputation policy manual actions work in the EEA from August 30. Outside the EEA, affected sections can still receive a direct manual-action impact. Inside the EEA, Google says that direct impact will not apply, while the affected section may be separated so it can rank independently over time.

The important point for SEO teams is not to treat this as permission to publish low-value third-party content on strong domains. Google has kept the policy. The enforcement outcome is what changes.

Publishers should review third-party content, partner sections, Search Console manual actions, and country-level performance before the change takes effect. FAQ

What is changing in Google's site reputation policy?

From August 30, 2026, site reputation policy manual actions will have different effects depending on whether the searcher is inside or outside the European Economic Area.

Will a site reputation manual action affect Google results in the EEA?

Google says the direct impact of the manual action will not apply to users inside the EEA. The affected section may instead be separated in Google's systems so it can rank independently over time.

Will the policy still affect users outside the EEA?

Yes. Google says that outside the EEA, a site reputation policy manual action will directly affect search results for the affected portion of a site.

Will Google Search Console still show manual actions?

Yes. Google says site owners will continue to receive Search Console notifications for manual actions. Reconsideration requests remain available, and eligible sites can also use mediation after a reconsideration request.
